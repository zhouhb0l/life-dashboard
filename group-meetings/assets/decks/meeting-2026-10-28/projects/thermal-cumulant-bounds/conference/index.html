Order one—response: “For two neighboring β nodes, −ΔβJ = Ji − Ji+1 ≥ 0. Because the left node is hotter, this says that heating the hot bath raises the current. Failure means the current falls as the thermal bias grows: negative differential thermal conductance, or NDTC, over that sampled interval. M14 passes, so there is no sampled NDTC here.”

Order two—shape: “For three equally spaced β nodes, Δβ2J = Ji − 2Ji+1 + Ji+2 ≥ 0. Geometrically, the middle current must lie below the chord joining its neighbors: J bends upward when plotted against β. A failure is a loss of β-convexity, or an inverse-temperature curvature anomaly. It can occur while J remains positive and while heating still increases J, so it is not automatically NDTC.”

Translate the curvature into temperature language: “For a smooth curve, ∂β2J = kB2Th2 d[Th2(∂J/∂Th)]/dTh. A finite-node convexity failure therefore implies that Th2(∂J/∂Th) decreases somewhere in the sampled interval. The differential conductance need not be negative there, so this is not automatically NDTC. Our targeted search found no broadly established standalone name for βh-curvature failure; ‘β-curvature anomaly’ is the precise descriptive term used here.”

Order three—hidden failure: “M14 passes direction, monotonicity, and β-convexity. Only the four-point quantity W3 = −Δβ3J fails. So the red card is not reversal, NDTC, or loss of convexity; it is the first hidden fixed-Landauer shape failure.”

Claim boundary and transition: “Passing the lower faces does not prove a Landauer model. It only shows that the familiar lower-order anomalies are absent at these sampled nodes. The next question is why a fixed passive transmission imposes the stronger hierarchy.”

Key words: null model · passive · fixed transmission · inverse temperature

Read the equation in words: “The current is a sum over bosonic frequencies. At each frequency, the transmission τ tells us how strongly that channel carries heat, and the bracket is the difference between the hot and cold Bose occupations.” The function nB is the Bose–Einstein occupation. The cold inverse temperature βc is fixed; only βh is varied.

Define the assumptions: Passive means τ(ω) ≥ 0: no channel actively supplies energy. Fixed means the same τ must explain all four temperatures; we are not allowed to refit it at every point. Stationary two-terminal means one hot bath, one cold bath, and a settled mean current. The order-zero sign is asserted on the hot domain 0 < βh ≤ βc.

Interpret a violation: If W3 < 0, there is no single nonnegative, temperature-independent τ in this representation that fits the four currents. Temperature-dependent contacts, saturation, an active or nonthermal bath, or inelastic/self-consistent transport could all fall outside the null. The test does not choose among them.

04 · our theory

Bose statistics hide a positive Laplace transform

That structure forces every signed derivative—and every signed divided difference—to have a fixed sign.

1Bose expansion
\[n_B(\beta\omega)=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-k\beta\omega}\]

Expand the temperature-dependent Bose factor.

→
2change variable
\[F(\beta)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\beta s}q(s)\,ds\]

Absorb the fixed cold contribution into a constant.

→
3positive density
\[q(s)=s\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\tau(s/k)}{k^2}\ge0\]

Passivity makes \(F\) completely monotone.

Theorem
\[(-1)^m[\beta_0,\ldots,\beta_m]J\ge0\qquad (m\ge1)\]

For equal spacing, divided differences reduce—up to a positive factor—to signed forward differences. Order zero is the separate hot-domain statement \(J\ge0\).

P10/theory/theorem.md · finite-node hierarchyNo numerical differentiation required

Key words: positive mixture · Laplace transform · complete monotonicity · divided difference

Give the intuition before the algebra: “A Bose occupation can be expanded as a sum of decaying exponentials. Because the transmission is nonnegative, every exponential enters with a nonnegative weight. Therefore the hot-dependent part of the current is a positive mixture of decays.” That positive mixture is what mathematicians call a Laplace transform of a nonnegative density.

Walk across the slide: Step 1 expands nB. Step 2 collects the exponentials into F(β). Step 3 shows the weight q(s) is nonnegative because τ is nonnegative. Such an F is completely monotone: its derivatives alternate signs. The measured current is J(βh) = F(βh) − F(βc); subtracting the fixed cold constant does not change any difference of order one or higher.

Define the bracket: [β0, …, βm]J is a divided difference, the finite-sample analogue of an m-th derivative. The theorem starts at m = 1. The m = 0 statement is separately J ≥ 0 on the hot domain.
